The moment the sliding doors part at Chalet Ivy Jozankei, the atmosphere shifts. Gone is the formal hush of a centuries-old ryokan; in its place is something warmer and more relaxed — golden timber walls, low-slung Scandinavian furniture, and the faint mineral scent of hot spring water drifting in from the bath wing. This is not a temple to tradition so much as a mountain chalet reinterpreted through a distinctly Hokkaido lens, built for travelers who want the therapeutic power of onsen bathing without the rigidity that sometimes comes with it. Nestled along the Toyohira River gorge in Jozankei, roughly an hour southwest of central Sapporo, Chalet Ivy occupies a sweet spot: close enough for a weekend escape from the city, remote enough to feel like true wilderness. The first impression is one of unhurried comfort — a lodge where ski-town coziness meets onsen ritual, and where the surrounding cedar and maple forest seems to press right up against the windows.

## The Birch Forest Roots of Jozankei

Jozankei's hot spring history stretches back to 1866, when the Buddhist priest Jozan Miizumi discovered the springs while trekking through what was then untouched Ezo wilderness. He is credited with opening the area to travelers, and the town today still bears his name as a quiet tribute. For over a century, Jozankei developed as Sapporo's "backyard onsen," a retreat where city dwellers escaped the summer heat or winter chill without a long journey. Chalet Ivy is a newer arrival to this landscape, built to reflect a more contemporary Hokkaido identity — one shaped by the region's dairy farms, ski culture, and Nordic-influenced design sensibility rather than the classical Kyoto or Kinosaki aesthetic. The "chalet" concept is deliberate: Hokkaido's climate and mountain terrain have long drawn comparisons to Scandinavia and the Alps, and this property leans into that connection with exposed beams, deep eaves for snow load, and a general architectural warmth suited to long, cold winters. It represents a newer chapter in Jozankei's story, proving the town can hold both centuries-old ritual and modern hospitality design side by side.

## Bathing in Toyohira River Mineral Water

The onsen at Chalet Ivy draws from Jozankei's signature sodium chloride and calcium chloride-rich springs, water long prized for its high salinity, which coats the skin in a fine mineral layer that helps retain body heat long after leaving the bath — a quality locals call "the keeping warmth" effect, especially valuable during Hokkaido's brutal winters. The water emerges from underground at a notably high temperature and is cooled through controlled circulation before reaching the tubs, arriving slightly cloudy with a faint saline note. The indoor baths are wrapped in pale hinoki-toned wood and stone, with tall windows angled to catch the Toyohira River gorge below, while the outdoor rotenburo is the property's true centerpiece: a stone-edged pool set directly against the tree line, where steam rises to meet the cold mountain air. In winter, snow gathers on the pines just beyond the bath's edge, creating a striking contrast between the heated water and the frozen forest — a scene many guests describe as the defining image of their stay. Spring and autumn transform the same view entirely, with fresh green canopy giving way to fiery maple and larch color by October. Private family baths are also available for reservation, ideal for those wanting an unhurried, unshared soak with the same forest outlook.

### Mineral Composition and Wellness Effects

Jozankei's chloride-type spring water is traditionally associated with relief for neuralgia, muscle fatigue, joint stiffness, and poor circulation — common complaints after a day of hiking or skiing in the surrounding hills. The mild alkalinity also leaves skin feeling notably smooth, a quality that has earned Jozankei's springs a loyal following among long-time Hokkaido residents who return seasonally.

## Timber, Wool, and Warm Light

Chalet Ivy's guest rooms depart from the classic tatami-and-shoji formula in favor of a hybrid design: Japanese-influenced restraint paired with the textural comfort of a European mountain lodge. Expect wide-plank flooring, wool throws, and low wooden furniture with soft, rounded edges, all bathed in warm amber lighting rather than the stark white common to business hotels. Many rooms incorporate a tatami-mat sleeping area alongside a small sitting nook, letting guests choose between futon bedding and a raised bed depending on the room category. Large windows are a priority throughout — the design team clearly understood that Jozankei's forested valley is itself the main decoration, and rooms are oriented to maximize river or mountain views wherever possible. Some higher-tier rooms include their own semi-open-air bath, allowing guests to soak privately while looking out over the birch canopy, a quiet luxury for those who prefer not to venture to the communal wing after dark. The overall wabi-sabi sensibility here is understated rather than austere: natural materials, muted earth tones, and a deliberate absence of clutter, creating rooms that feel less like a hotel and more like a friend's well-appointed cabin.

## A Hokkaido Kaiseki Table

Dinner at Chalet Ivy is built around Hokkaido's exceptional local produce, and the kaiseki course reflects the island's reputation as Japan's culinary larder. Expect a procession of small, seasonal dishes: sashimi featuring Hokkaido scallops, uni, or salmon depending on the month, followed by a simmered course of local vegetables like Jozankei-grown pumpkin or mountain vegetables (sansai) foraged nearby. A tabletop hot pot often anchors the meal, frequently built around Hokkaido's famous milk-fed pork or seasonal crab, allowing guests to cook ingredients tableside while the broth deepens in flavor. Grilled courses might showcase Hokkaido beef or a whole seasonal fish, finished simply to let the ingredient's quality speak for itself, while a small course of Ishikari-style salmon and vegetable stew occasionally appears as a nod to regional tradition. Meals conclude with a light dessert, often incorporating Hokkaido's renowned dairy — a soft-serve or milk pudding that showcases the region's famously rich, high-fat milk. Breakfast the next morning shifts to a more casual register: a set tray with grilled fish, rice, miso soup, pickles, and a small salad, alongside optional additions like a soft-cooked egg or Hokkaido yogurt, giving guests a gentle, grounding start before a day of exploring.

## Beyond the Baths: Exploring Sapporo's Backyard

Jozankei's location makes it an ideal base for combining onsen relaxation with active exploration. The Toyohira River gorge offers scenic walking trails, particularly stunning during the autumn foliage season when maple and larch trees turn the valley into a corridor of red and gold. Nearby, the Jozankei Dam and its surrounding reservoir provide quiet lakeside walks, while more ambitious travelers can hike sections of Mount Sapporo-dake for panoramic views back toward the city. Winter transforms the area into a snow sports hub — several ski resorts sit within a short drive, making Chalet Ivy a natural pairing for a ski-and-soak itinerary. For those wanting a change of pace, the town center has small craft shops and cafes serving Hokkaido-grown coffee and dairy treats. A day trip into central Sapporo is easy from here, opening up access to the Sapporo Beer Museum, Odori Park, and — depending on season — the Sapporo Snow Festival in February, one of Japan's most spectacular winter events.

## Booking Smart and Bathing Respectfully

Tattoos remain a sensitive topic at most traditional Japanese baths, and while policies vary by season and staffing, guests with visible tattoos should contact the ryokan directly before booking or inquire about private family bath reservations, which sidestep the issue entirely. The best seasons to visit are winter (December through February) for the dramatic snow-and-steam contrast in the outdoor bath, and autumn (late September through October) for the valley's renowned foliage — both tend to book out early, so reserving two to three months ahead is strongly advised. Summer offers a cooler, greener alternative to Sapporo's own mild but occasionally humid season, and is a good option for travelers seeking lower rates and a quieter bath experience. When booking, note that room categories with private open-air baths sell out fastest and often represent the best value for couples or those prioritizing privacy over communal bathing.

## Getting to Jozankei from Sapporo

Chalet Ivy sits within Jozankei Onsen, roughly 50 to 60 minutes by car from New Chitose Airport or central Sapporo via Route 230, a scenic drive that climbs gradually into the mountains south of the city. Travelers without a rental car can take a Jotetsu bus directly from Sapporo Station or Makomanai Station on the Sapporo Municipal Subway, with the journey taking approximately 60 to 70 minutes depending on traffic and stops. Many guests choose to combine the trip with a day in Sapporo itself, treating Jozankei as a restorative bookend to a city-focused Hokkaido itinerary. Shuttle service or pickup arrangements should be confirmed directly with the property, as options can vary by season, particularly during Hokkaido's heavy snow months when road conditions demand extra planning.
