## A Mountain Retreat on the Toyohira River

Tucked into a steep volcanic gorge where the Toyohira River carves through cedar-covered slopes, **Suizantei** feels like a secret kept just far enough from Sapporo to still be a secret. Drive forty minutes south of the city's bustling Susukino district and the skyscrapers dissolve into forested ridgelines, the air sharpens, and the scent of sulfur begins to drift through open car windows. This is Jozankei Onsen, often called "Sapporo's Backyard Hot Spring," and Suizantei sits proudly along its riverbank as one of the area's most atmospheric ryokan.

What strikes first-time guests is the sound before anything else — the rush of the Toyohira River against granite boulders, audible from the lobby, the corridors, and especially the open-air baths. Suizantei's architecture leans into this setting rather than away from it: large windows, stone pathways, and a lobby that frames the gorge like a living painting. It is not a flashy property. Instead, it trades spectacle for intimacy, offering a quieter, more contemplative version of the Hokkaido onsen experience that still feels worlds away despite its proximity to a major city.

## The Samurai's Hidden Spring: A Brief History of Jozankei

Jozankei's origin story is steeped in local legend. According to town history, the hot spring was discovered in 1866 by a Buddhist priest named Miizumi Jozan, who stumbled upon the steaming waters while exploring the then-unmapped valley. His name lives on in the town itself — "Jozan-kei," meaning "Jozan's Valley." For decades afterward, the onsen remained a remote retreat accessible only by rough mountain trails, favored by woodsmen and travelers seeking relief from Hokkaido's brutal winters.

It was not until the early 20th century, with the arrival of railways and later paved roads connecting the valley to Sapporo, that Jozankei blossomed into a full-fledged resort town. Ryokan like Suizantei emerged from this era of growth, built specifically to showcase the gorge-side setting that had drawn bathers for generations. While Suizantei itself represents a more contemporary incarnation of the ryokan tradition, it carries forward Jozankei's defining ethos: hospitality rooted in the raw, untamed beauty of the river valley, rather than in manufactured luxury. The town's development as a retreat for Sapporo's elite and ordinary citizens alike has given Jozankei a reputation as the "onsen next door" for Hokkaido's largest city — accessible, yet never losing its wilderness character.

## Soaking in Alkaline Simple Hot Springs: The Baths of Suizantei

The heart of any stay at Suizantei is, unsurprisingly, its bathing facilities, and here the ryokan delivers an experience defined by both water chemistry and visual drama. The spring water is classified as a **weakly alkaline simple hot spring** (tanjun-sen), prized throughout Japan for being gentle on the skin while still delivering the signature warmth and mineral benefits onsen bathers seek. Unlike the more pungent sulfur baths found elsewhere in Hokkaido, Jozankei's waters are colorless, nearly odorless, and renowned for a silky, almost slippery texture against the skin — locals often call this quality "bihada no yu," or "beautiful skin water."

Suizantei's open-air rotenburo are positioned directly along the Toyohira gorge, meaning bathers soak while gazing across the rushing river toward dense stands of Hokkaido oak, maple, and katsura trees. In autumn, this view erupts into fiery reds and oranges, making the baths a prime destination for leaf-peeping travelers. In winter, steam rises off the water into falling snow, with the dark evergreens and white riverbanks creating a striking monochrome backdrop. Indoor baths are also available for those seeking warmth without wind exposure, and most are arranged with large picture windows that preserve the gorge view even when bathing indoors. Many rooms additionally offer private in-room baths, allowing couples or families to enjoy the mineral waters with complete privacy.

## Wabi-Sabi Comfort: Rooms Designed for Stillness

Suizantei's guest rooms embrace a restrained, wabi-sabi-inflected aesthetic that favors natural materials over ornamentation. Tatami flooring, pale wood paneling, and shoji screens diffuse the daylight into a soft glow, while low furniture and futon bedding keep sightlines open toward the river views beyond the window. The goal is a kind of intentional quietude — nothing in the room competes with the sound of the water outside.

Room categories range from standard Japanese-style tatami rooms to more spacious suites that pair a traditional sleeping area with a modern seating nook, and premium options featuring private open-air baths (rotenburo-tsuki) overlooking the gorge. These private-bath rooms are especially popular among honeymooners and anniversary travelers, as they allow guests to soak at any hour without venturing to the communal facilities. Subtle Hokkaido touches appear throughout — woven textiles, locally crafted pottery used for welcome tea service, and in some rooms, views that stretch toward the forested ridge where wild deer are occasionally spotted at dawn.

## A Hokkaido Kaiseki Feast: Dinner and Breakfast at Suizantei

Dining at Suizantei is built around a kaiseki-style multi-course dinner that showcases Hokkaido's exceptional seasonal bounty — one of the island's great culinary advantages. Expect a procession of small, meticulously plated dishes: an opening sakizuke of seasonal vegetables and delicate dashi jelly, followed by sashimi featuring Hokkaido staples such as hotate (scallop), uni, or seasonal salmon, often sourced from nearby coastal ports. A simmered dish (nimono) might showcase root vegetables and Hokkaido-raised pork or beef, while a course of grilled fish — frequently kinki or hokke — arrives glistening with a light soy glaze.

Hokkaido's dairy and agricultural reputation also makes an appearance, with courses sometimes incorporating local corn, potatoes, or even a touch of Hokkaido butter in a finishing sauce. A hot pot course is common in colder months, built around crab, salmon, or seasonal vegetables simmered tableside in a clay pot — a comforting ritual after a long soak in the baths. Meals typically conclude with rice steamed in the Hokkaido style, miso soup, pickles, and a seasonal dessert, often a melon or berry sorbet reflecting the prefecture's famous fruit harvests.

Breakfast shifts toward a more relaxed but equally generous set menu, pairing grilled fish, Japanese omelet, rice, miso soup, and small side dishes with regional touches like Hokkaido yogurt or fresh milk — a quiet nod to the surrounding dairy country that makes for an energizing start before a day of sightseeing.

## Beyond the Baths: Exploring Sapporo and the Jozankei Valley

Suizantei's location makes it an excellent base for exploring both the natural valley and Sapporo's urban attractions. Within the Jozankei area itself, the **Jozankei Dam** and its surrounding walking trails offer scenic hikes with sweeping views of the reservoir and mountains, particularly spectacular during the autumn foliage season from late September through mid-October. The **Futami Suspension Bridge**, with its dramatic view of twin rock formations rising from the river, is a short drive from the ryokan and a popular photo stop.

Nature lovers should consider a visit to **Hoheikyo Onsen** area trails or the nearby **Jozankei Farm**, where seasonal fruit picking and animal encounters appeal to families. For those craving the city, Sapporo itself is roughly a 45-minute drive away, home to the Sapporo Clock Tower, Odori Park, the historic Sapporo Beer Museum, and the famous Nijo Market for fresh seafood. Winter visitors often combine a Suizantei stay with a trip to nearby ski resorts or the Sapporo Snow Festival, held each February, making Jozankei a perfect quiet counterpoint to a day spent on the slopes or amid festival crowds.

## Before You Soak: Tattoos, Seasons, and Booking Wisdom

Like many traditional ryokan, Suizantei maintains a conventional **no-visible-tattoo policy** for communal bathing areas, a holdover from historical associations between tattoos and organized crime in Japan. Travelers with tattoos should inquire directly about private in-room bath options, which sidestep this restriction entirely and are often the best solution regardless of the policy.

Seasonally, Jozankei rewards visits in both **autumn** (late September to mid-October) for the valley's spectacular foliage, and **winter** (December through February) for snow-draped rotenburo bathing, widely considered one of Hokkaido's most romantic experiences. Spring and early summer bring fewer crowds and lush greenery, along with lower rates. Booking tips: reserve rooms with private baths well in advance, especially around Golden Week, Obon, and the Sapporo Snow Festival in February, when both Sapporo and Jozankei fill quickly. Weekday stays typically offer better rates and more room availability than weekends.

## Getting to Suizantei: The Journey from Sapporo

Suizantei is most easily reached from **New Chitose Airport** or central Sapporo. From Sapporo Station or Susukino, travelers can take a direct bus bound for Jozankei Onsen, operated by Jotetsu Bus, with a journey time of roughly 60–70 minutes depending on traffic and stop. Many guests opt for taxi or rental car instead, as the drive from central Sapporo takes approximately 45 minutes via Route 230, winding gradually upward into the valley with increasingly scenic mountain views.

For those arriving from New Chitose Airport, a taxi or private transfer takes around 70–90 minutes, while a combination of airport bus to Sapporo Station followed by a Jozankei-bound bus is the most economical route, if slightly longer. Many ryokan in the area, including Suizantei, can arrange shuttle pickup from Sapporo Station with advance reservation — a convenient option for travelers without a rental car, letting the stresses of the city fall away well before arrival at the riverside gates.
