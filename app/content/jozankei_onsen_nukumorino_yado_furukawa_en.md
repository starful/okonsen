Arriving at Nukumorino Yado Furukawa feels like slipping through a seam in the city. Sapporo's grid of department stores and ramen alleys recedes within forty-five minutes, replaced by cedar-dark mountains and the percussive rush of the Toyohira River. The ryokan itself is modest in scale compared to the sprawling resort hotels that dominate much of Jozankei, and that is precisely its appeal. "Nukumori" means warmth, and the name is not marketing fluff — it describes the actual sensation of walking through the lobby's low lighting and cedar scent into a space that feels handled, cared for, and personal rather than processed. This is a ryokan for travelers who want the restorative power of hot spring bathing without the convention-hall energy of a 300-room resort.

## A River Town's Quiet Corner

Jozankei's onsen history stretches back to 1866, when the Buddhist monk Jozan Miizumi, exhausted from mountain ascetic practice, discovered the hot springs bubbling along the Toyohira gorge and began guiding sick travelers there for healing. The town grew slowly around this reputation for curative waters, eventually becoming known as "Sapporo's Backyard Hot Spring" for its proximity to the prefectural capital. Furukawa itself belongs to a newer generation of Jozankei lodging — smaller, independently run inns that emerged as counterpoints to the large bus-tour hotels built during Japan's postwar tourism boom. Rather than competing on scale, properties like Furukawa lean into omotenashi hospitality: fewer rooms, more attentive service, and a deliberate effort to let the river and forest remain the visual protagonist rather than being obscured by hotel infrastructure. The inn's name, tied to the Furukawa family stewardship, signals generational continuity — a commitment to maintaining the ryokan's intimate character even as Jozankei around it modernizes.

## The Gorge-View Baths and Mineral Waters

The soul of any stay here is the bathing experience, and Furukawa's baths are built to maximize the drama of their riverside setting. The source water is a sodium-chloride and calcium hot spring, locally referred to for its skin-softening qualities — bathers often describe the water as "nururi," a slightly silky texture that coats the skin and is said to lock in moisture after bathing, a quality historically prized by Hokkaido travelers enduring harsh winters. The mineral content also carries mild therapeutic associations for muscle fatigue and poor circulation, making it a popular stop for hikers coming down from nearby peaks.

### The Rotenburo Experience

The open-air bath (rotenburo) is positioned to look directly over the Toyohira River gorge, where steam rises off the water's surface and mixes with mist coming off the rapids below. In autumn, maple and oak canopies burn orange and crimson directly across the ravine; in winter, snow accumulates on exposed rock faces while the bath itself steams defiantly against the cold, creating the classic "yukimi-buro" snow-viewing bath aesthetic that draws visitors from across Asia. The sound of the river is a constant, almost percussive backdrop — guests frequently note that it functions as natural white noise, deepening the sense of relaxation far more than a static, silent bath could.

### Indoor Baths and Private Options

The indoor bath areas are tiled in warm stone tones and kept at a slightly gentler temperature than the outdoor pools, giving bathers a place to acclimate before stepping into the cooler mountain air outside. For couples, families, or guests seeking total privacy, the ryokan offers reservable private baths (kashikiri), which allow unhurried access to the gorge view without sharing the space — a particularly valued option for those with tattoos who might otherwise feel excluded from communal facilities.

## Wabi-Sabi Rooms Framed by Forest

Furukawa's guest rooms favor understated texture over flashy luxury. Traditional tatami rooms are furnished with low chabudai tables, futon bedding laid out fresh each evening, and shoji screens that filter the forest light into a soft diffusion throughout the day. The aesthetic leans into wabi-sabi principles: natural wood grain left visible rather than lacquered to a high shine, ceramics with asymmetric glazing, and an overall restraint that lets the river view through the window do the decorative work. Many rooms face the gorge directly, meaning guests wake to the same rushing water sound that accompanies their evening bath — an intentional design choice that threads the river motif through the entire stay rather than confining it to the bathing areas. Rooms with river-facing balconies or attached semi-open-air baths command a premium but are worth the splurge for travelers prioritizing privacy and uninterrupted nature immersion.

## A Kaiseki Table Built on Hokkaido's Harvest

Dinner at Furukawa is where Hokkaido's reputation as Japan's most abundant larder comes fully into focus. The kaiseki course typically opens with a seasonal sakizuke appetizer — perhaps a small dish of uni or ikura sourced from nearby coastal waters — followed by a succession of courses built around Hokkaido's signature ingredients: tender Jingisukan-style lamb or wagyu beef seared tableside on a hoba leaf or sizzling stone, delicate slices of sashimi featuring scallops and salmon from Hokkaido's surrounding seas, and a hot pot (nabe) laden with local vegetables, mushrooms, and often crab legs cracked open at the table. Seasonal vegetables from nearby Jozankei farms appear in simmered dishes (nimono) that showcase the subtle dashi-based broths central to Japanese home-style cooking elevated to ryokan standards. The meal typically closes with a small rice dish, miso soup, pickles, and a seasonal dessert — often a melon or berry compote reflecting Hokkaido's famed produce.

Breakfast the following morning is comparatively simple but no less considered: grilled fish, rice, miso soup, a soft-cooked egg, and an assortment of small side dishes meant to settle the stomach gently after a soak, rather than overwhelm it before a day of sightseeing.

## Beyond the Ryokan: Exploring Sapporo's Backyard

Jozankei's location makes it an ideal base for both nature exploration and city access. The Jozankei Dai-ichi Dam and surrounding hiking trails offer accessible walks with river and gorge views, particularly spectacular during the autumn foliage season from late September through October. Nearby Hoheikyo Onsen and the broader Shikotsu-Toya National Park boundary offer additional outdoor excursions for travelers extending their stay. For those wanting a change of pace, the short drive or bus ride back into central Sapporo opens up the Sapporo Beer Museum, Odori Park, and the Nijo Market for fresh seafood — meaning guests can combine deep rural relaxation with urban culture in the same trip without needing to relocate hotels. Winter visitors should note Jozankei's proximity to Sapporo's ski fields, making Furukawa a comfortable soak-and-ski base during the snow season.

## Planning Your Stay

### Tattoo Policy

Like many traditional ryokan, Furukawa's communal baths generally maintain a no-visible-tattoo policy rooted in longstanding onsen culture. Travelers with tattoos should not be discouraged, however — the private kashikiri baths are the clear workaround, allowing full enjoyment of the gorge-view soak without needing to cover ink or forgo bathing altogether. Always confirm current policy directly with the ryokan when booking, as rules can vary by season and ownership.

### Best Season to Visit

Autumn (late September to mid-October) delivers the most visually striking combination of foliage and steam, while winter (December through February) offers the classic snow-bathing experience many travelers associate with Hokkaido onsens. Summer provides a cooler, greener alternative to Honshu's heat, making Jozankei a popular escape for travelers fleeing humid southern cities.

### Booking Hacks

Book river-facing or private-bath rooms well in advance, particularly for autumn foliage weekends when Hokkaido domestic tourism peaks. Weekday stays often come with notably lower rates and better room availability than weekend slots, and booking directly through the ryokan rather than third-party aggregators sometimes unlocks early check-in or late checkout perks unavailable through standard booking platforms.

## Getting There

From New Chitose Airport, the most straightforward route is a direct airport limousine bus to Jozankei Onsen, a journey of roughly 90 minutes depending on traffic and season. Travelers arriving via Sapporo Station can take a direct Jozankei-bound bus from the Sapporo bus terminal, typically taking 60 to 70 minutes and dropping passengers within walking distance of the ryokan's entrance. Those renting a car should anticipate a scenic but occasionally winding mountain drive, particularly manageable outside of winter snowfall; snow tires or careful driving are essential for self-drivers visiting between December and March. Many guests simplify logistics by arranging a private transfer or taxi directly from the ryokan upon booking, especially useful for travelers carrying luggage for an onward Hokkaido itinerary.
