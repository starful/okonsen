Stepping off the narrow approach road into Adzuma's entrance, the first sensation is smell before sight: a faint, mineral tang of sulphur drifting on cold Hokkaido air, carried down from the steaming crater valley above. Where many Noboribetsu establishments lean into scale and spectacle, Adzuma leans the opposite direction — toward hush, dim wood tones, and the sound of running spring water. It is a ryokan built for people who came to Noboribetsu for one reason only: the water itself. The lobby is modest, almost austere, with low lighting and a single ikebana arrangement doing all the talking. There is no sprawling buffet hall greeting you, no arcade of souvenir counters. Instead, staff in dark indigo aprons quietly direct you toward check-in, already anticipating that most guests want to be undressed and submerged within the hour.

## A Spa Born of the Jigokudani Valley

Noboribetsu's onsen history stretches back to the Ainu people, who regarded the smoking valley of Jigokudani — literally "Hell Valley" — as a place of both danger and healing. By the Meiji era, Japanese settlers and physicians had identified the area's extraordinary spring diversity, and small inns began appearing along the slope leading up from the valley floor. Adzuma traces its own lineage to this early wave of development, though the current building has been rebuilt and renovated across generations while preserving its original siting: close enough to the valley's fissures to draw water with minimal piping, yet set back enough to avoid the harsher fumes. Older regulars speak of the ryokan's name itself — "Adzuma," an old term once used for the eastern frontier — as a quiet nod to Hokkaido's identity as Japan's northern edge, a place settled later and differently than the onsen towns of Honshu. That frontier character still shows in the ryokan's unpretentious service style: direct, warm, without the theatrical formality sometimes found in Kyoto or Hakone establishments.

## Nine Springs, One Valley: The Baths of Adzuma

What sets Noboribetsu apart from almost every other onsen town in Japan is the sheer variety of spring chemistries emerging from a single caldera, and Adzuma makes a point of showcasing this diversity rather than funneling everything into one generic pool. The flagship bath draws from a **sulphur spring** (iō-sen), instantly recognizable by its cloudy, pale jade-white water and the distinct rotten-egg aroma that onsen devotees learn to love. This water is prized for its effects on skin conditions and circulation, and bathers will notice a faint silky film on the skin after soaking — a sign of the colloidal sulphur suspended in the liquid.

Alongside it, Adzuma channels a **sodium chloride spring** (ensen), clear and markedly warming, often called the "finishing-touch" bath because its heat-retention properties keep the body warm long after toweling off, ideal during Hokkaido's brutal winters. A smaller indoor bath draws on an **iron-rich spring**, carrying a faint rust tint and a mineral taste in the steam, traditionally recommended for anemia and fatigue recovery.

### The Rotenburo and Its View

The outdoor bath, or rotenburo, is where Adzuma earns its reputation. Carved partially into natural rock and screened by cedar and dwarf bamboo, the open-air pool looks directly up toward the ridge that cradles Jigokudani, with steam vents visible rising from the valley floor on clear mornings. In winter, snow accumulates on the rock ledges inches from the water's edge, creating the signature Hokkaido contrast of scalding mineral water against freezing air — guests often stay in far longer than intended simply to keep extending the view. There is no artificial lighting at night beyond a few low lanterns, so stargazing in the rotenburo on a clear, cold evening is one of the ryokan's quiet signature experiences.

## Rooms Shaped by Northern Simplicity

Adzuma's guest rooms reject showy design in favor of what might be called northern wabi-sabi: unfinished cedar beams, tatami in muted grey-green tones, and paper shoji screens that filter rather than block the valley light. Furnishings are kept deliberately sparse — a low chabudai table, a single hanging scroll, cushions instead of over-furnished seating — so that nothing distracts from the alcove window framing birch and pine outside. Several rooms include a private half-open-air bath (kashikiri-buro), fed by the same spring lines as the communal baths, allowing couples or families to soak in the mineral water without leaving the room. Heating is notably robust, a necessary architectural priority given Hokkaido's long, severe winters, and the futon bedding is thicker than typical Honshu ryokan standards. Corner rooms on upper floors often catch a partial valley view, worth requesting specifically at booking.

## Kaiseki Built on Hokkaido's Cold Waters

Dinner at Adzuma is a multi-course kaiseki meal anchored firmly in Hokkaido's exceptional seafood economy rather than generic Japanese formality. Expect a procession beginning with a delicate sakizuke appetizer — perhaps a small dish of marinated **hairy crab** (kegani) or sea urchin (uni), both local specialties pulled from the cold Pacific waters off the Hokkaido coast. The sashimi course typically rotates seasonally between scallop, salmon, and squid, each prized for the clean, cold-water sweetness that distinguishes Hokkaido fish from warmer southern catches.

A simmered course often features **Yubari melon**-fed or locally raised pork, or a rich miso-based hotpot incorporating root vegetables harvested from the volcanic soil nearby — Hokkaido's famous potatoes, corn, and pumpkin showing up throughout the meal in subtly sweet, earthy notes. A grilled course frequently showcases salmon or seasonal mountain vegetables (sansai) over charcoal, finished with a simple citrus-soy glaze that lets the ingredient speak for itself. Breakfast the next morning shifts tone entirely: a far simpler, rice-centered spread with grilled fish, pickles, miso soup, and a soft-cooked egg, meant to settle the stomach after the previous night's richness and prepare guests for another long soak before checkout.

## Beyond the Baths: Exploring Noboribetsu

Jigokudani itself is Noboribetsu's unmissable centerpiece, a short walk from most ryokan including Adzuma, where boardwalks wind along the rim of the steaming, sulphur-stained crater — most striking at dusk when the valley glows faintly orange. The nearby **Noboribetsu Bear Park**, reached by ropeway, houses Hokkaido brown bears and offers panoramic views over the valley and, on clear days, the Pacific coastline beyond. Oyunuma Pond, a short hike from the valley, is a genuinely boiling sulphur lake ringed by a forest walking trail, with a foot-bath stream (Oyunumagawa Natenseki) where visitors can soak tired feet in naturally heated river water for free. Evening visitors should consider the "Yukake Jizo Jigoku Matsuri" street performances during summer months, when the town stages playful oni (demon) parades referencing the valley's "hell" imagery — a lighthearted counterpoint to the otherwise serene onsen atmosphere.

## Practical Wisdom Before You Book

Tattoo policies at Adzuma follow the conservative norm common across Hokkaido ryokan: visible tattoos are generally not permitted in communal baths, though covering stickers are sometimes available at the front desk, and guests with concerns should inquire directly when booking. The most rewarding season is arguably winter (December through February), when heavy snowfall transforms the rotenburo into a dramatic contrast of ice and steam, though this is also peak demand, so reservations should be made two to three months in advance. Early autumn (late September to October) offers crisp air and turning leaves around the valley rim at a quieter pace and often better rates. For booking hacks, mid-week stays (Tuesday through Thursday) tend to run noticeably cheaper than weekend rates, and some travel agencies bundle Adzuma with a Noboribetsu Bear Park ropeway ticket at a discount — worth checking before booking baths and attractions separately.

## Getting to Adzuma

The most common route begins at New Chitose Airport, from which a direct limousine bus runs to Noboribetsu Onsen, taking roughly 80 to 100 minutes depending on traffic and stops. Travelers coming by rail should take the JR Hokuto or Suzuran limited express from Sapporo Station to Noboribetsu Station, a journey of about 50 to 70 minutes, followed by a short local bus or taxi ride (around 15 minutes) up into the onsen town itself, since the station sits on the coast below the hot spring district. Many ryokan, including Adzuma, offer scheduled shuttle pickups from Noboribetsu Station at set times — confirming this shuttle in advance saves considerable hassle with luggage on the steep approach road. Renting a car from Sapporo or New Chitose is also a popular option, giving flexibility to stop at Lake Shikotsu or coastal viewpoints en route, with the drive itself taking roughly 90 minutes under normal conditions.
