Arriving at Kuramure feels less like checking into a ryokan and more like stepping into a quiet, sculptural gallery that happens to be built around hot spring water. Tucked into a bend of the Toyohira River within Jozankei Onsen, roughly an hour's drive from downtown Sapporo, Kuramure immediately distinguishes itself from the timber-and-tatami convention of rural Japanese inns. Concrete walls, dim corridors lit by recessed lanterns, and an entrance that opens onto a hushed, library-like lounge set the tone: this is an onsen experience designed for adults seeking stillness, not spectacle. The first impression is one of restraint — low lighting, the murmur of the river outside, the faint mineral scent of sulfur drifting from somewhere unseen. It's a place that rewards slowing down.

## A Gorge Town Reimagined Through Modern Design

Jozankei itself has a history stretching back to 1866, when the Buddhist priest Miizumi Jozan discovered the springs while exploring the Toyohira gorge and began guiding travelers to its healing waters — the onsen town still carries his name. For over a century, Jozankei developed as a classic hot spring resort town serving Sapporo, dotted with large-scale hotels catering to tour groups. Kuramure arrived as something of a quiet rebellion against that model. Opened in the 2000s as a boutique alternative, it was conceived by its owners as an homage to "kura" (storehouses) and the idea of a treasury — a small, carefully curated repository of comfort, art, and water, rather than a sprawling resort. The name itself evokes a gathering of storehouses, and the architecture leans into that idea with its compartmentalized, almost monastic structure of individual volumes connected by shadowed passageways.

### The Toyohira River as a Constant Companion

Unlike many onsen towns where the river is incidental scenery, at Kuramure the Toyohira River is practically a co-architect. The ryokan is built directly along its banks, and nearly every design decision — window placement, bath orientation, the positioning of lounge seating — seems to negotiate with the water's movement and sound.

## Deep Dive into the Baths

Jozankei's onsen water is classified primarily as a sodium-calcium chloride spring, slightly alkaline and clear, prized for its excellent heat retention — locals have long called it "the hot spring that keeps you warm until morning." The high salt content coats the skin, trapping warmth, while trace minerals are said to ease muscle fatigue, joint stiffness, and poor circulation, making it especially welcome after a day of hiking or skiing in the surrounding mountains.

### Private Baths in Every Room

What sets Kuramure apart is its commitment to in-room bathing. Every guest room is paired with its own open-air or semi-open-air bath, typically carved from stone or finished in dark tile, positioned to frame views of the forested gorge or a private, moss-softened garden. Guests can soak at any hour without ever donning a yukata for a communal walk, which makes Kuramure particularly appealing for honeymooners, those with tattoos, or travelers who simply prefer unhurried, solitary bathing. The water in these baths is genuinely sourced from the Jozankei springs rather than recirculated, piped fresh to maintain the slightly saline, silky texture the region is known for.

### The Communal Bathing Spaces

Beyond the in-room baths, Kuramure maintains a shared bathing area with an indoor pool and an outdoor rotenburo positioned to catch the sound of the river below and, depending on the room category and season, glimpses of birch and maple framing the gorge. The communal bath is smaller and more intimate than the cavernous facilities typical of larger Jozankei hotels — consistent with the ryokan's overall philosophy of quality over scale. Steam rises against the backdrop of forested slopes that turn brilliant crimson and gold in autumn and sit under thick snow blankets in winter, making the same bath feel like an entirely different experience across seasons.

## Rooms & Architecture: Wabi-Sabi Through a Modern Lens

Kuramure's architecture rejects the ornate, gilded aesthetic some Japanese inns adopt in favor of something closer to a wabi-sabi-inflected modernist retreat. Rooms are finished in earthen plaster, dark wood, and raw stone, with furnishings that favor clean lines and negative space over decoration. Many rooms feature a sunken lounge area with low furniture, a fireplace or wood-burning stove in cooler months, and large windows that frame the river or forest like a living painting rather than simply letting in light.

### Curated Simplicity

Each room type carries a distinct character — some built around a cedar-lined private bath, others with a stone soaking tub positioned beside floor-to-ceiling glass. Beds tend toward Western-style comfort rather than traditional futon-on-tatami, a deliberate concession to contemporary guest expectations without sacrificing the overall sense of Japanese restraint. Textiles are kept muted — charcoal, indigo, unbleached linen — reinforcing the sense that the room itself is secondary to the experience of the water and the view beyond the glass.

## Gastronomy: A Kaiseki Shaped by Hokkaido's Bounty

Dinner at Kuramure follows the kaiseki tradition but filtered unmistakably through Hokkaido's exceptional local produce. Expect a procession of small, seasonal courses built around the island's famous seafood — uni, scallops, crab, and seasonal fish from the waters off Otaru and Hakodate — alongside mountain vegetables (sansai) foraged from the hills surrounding Jozankei. A sakizuke appetizer might arrive as a single, precisely arranged bite of marinated seafood or vegetable, followed by a clear dashi-based suimono soup meant to cleanse the palate before heavier courses.

### The Seasonal Procession

Autumn menus often lean into mushrooms, chestnuts, and salmon, reflecting Hokkaido's harvest, while winter dinners showcase crab hot pots and warming nabe dishes suited to the deep island cold. A grilled course might feature Hokkaido beef or a whole fish cooked over binchotan charcoal, and the meal typically closes with a modest rice course using local short-grain rice, pickles, and miso soup, followed by a delicate seasonal dessert. Meals are often served in-room or in a private dining alcove rather than a communal hall, preserving the same hushed, intimate atmosphere found throughout the rest of the property.

### Breakfast as a Quiet Reset

Breakfast is comparatively simple but no less considered — grilled fish, a soft-cooked or rolled tamagoyaki, rice, miso soup, and an assortment of small seasonal dishes designed to be restorative rather than extravagant, ideal fuel before a morning soak or a drive into Sapporo.

## Local Attractions Around Sapporo

Jozankei's proximity to Sapporo — about 45 minutes to an hour by car — makes Kuramure a practical base for exploring both nature and city in a single trip. Nearby, the Jozankei Dam and Hoheikyo Dam offer scenic walking routes, particularly stunning during the autumn foliage season when the gorge turns deep red and gold. The Niseko and Sapporo Kokusai ski resorts are within reach for winter visitors chasing Hokkaido's famous powder snow, while summer travelers can explore nearby hiking trails into the Shikotsu-Toya National Park.

### City Contrasts

A short drive back toward Sapporo opens up the city's own attractions — the Sapporo Beer Museum, the historic Clock Tower, Odori Park, and, depending on season, the Sapporo Snow Festival in February. Pairing a stay at Kuramure with a day or two in the city creates a natural rhythm: urban energy followed by total quiet immersion in the gorge.

## Practical Tips for Planning Your Stay

Kuramure, like most small boutique ryokans, generally does not permit guests with visible tattoos in its communal baths, though the abundance of private in-room baths makes this largely a non-issue for tattooed travelers — simply soak privately rather than in the shared rotenburo. The best seasons to visit are autumn (late September through October) for the gorge's foliage, and winter (December through February) for snow-draped scenery and the contrast of steaming baths against falling snow. Summer offers lush greenery and cooler temperatures than mainland Japan, a welcome escape from Honshu's humidity.

### Booking Considerations

Because Kuramure is small and popular among both domestic travelers and design-conscious international visitors, rooms — particularly those with the most sought-after river views — should be booked several weeks to months in advance, especially around Golden Week, Obon, and the Sapporo Snow Festival. Dinner timing and room-type selection are usually finalized at booking, so decide in advance whether a private open-air bath or an indoor cedar tub suits your preferences.

## Access Guide: Getting to Jozankei from Sapporo

The most comfortable route from New Chitose Airport or central Sapporo is by car or taxi, with the drive taking roughly 60 to 90 minutes depending on traffic and starting point. For those without a car, Chuo Bus operates regular services from Sapporo Station and Makomanai Station directly to Jozankei Onsen, with the journey taking approximately 60 to 70 minutes; many ryokans, including Kuramure, can arrange shuttle pickup from the bus terminal with advance notice. Travelers connecting from New Chitose Airport should plan for a transfer via Sapporo or a direct limousine bus where available, making an early afternoon departure ideal for arriving in time to enjoy a sunset soak before dinner.
