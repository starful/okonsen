## Where the Water Meets the Volcano: An Introduction to the Property

Few resorts in Japan occupy a setting as cinematic as The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko. Perched directly on the shoreline of Lake Ashinoko, a crater lake formed roughly three thousand years ago by the eruption of the Hakone volcanic complex, the hotel offers something that postcards struggle to capture: the sensation of standing between still water and an active, breathing landscape, with Mount Fuji rising on clear mornings as though placed there deliberately for the view. The property sits within Hakone-machi, a town that has spent centuries perfecting the art of welcoming travelers who arrive tired from the capital and leave restored.

Unlike many countryside ryokan that trade convenience for seclusion, The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko manages to feel both. It is close enough to Tokyo to be reached in under two hours by train and bus, yet the grounds themselves stretch across a private forest of roughly 400,000 square meters, giving guests the rare experience of a five-star resort that also functions as a nature reserve. Cedar and pine line the walking paths, the Hakone Detached Palace Garden borders the property, and the lake itself laps quietly against a private dock used for lakeside strolls and seasonal boat viewing.

The resort is composed of several distinct accommodation wings — the main Prince tower, the garden-facing Ryuguden wing, and a scattering of Western-style lakeside villas — each positioned to maximize either Fuji sightlines or direct lake frontage. This layered design means that a family celebrating a milestone anniversary and a solo traveler seeking a quiet onsen weekend can both find a room suited to their pace, without ever feeling like they are staying at the same hotel for different reasons. For travelers researching Hakone specifically for The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko, the appeal usually comes down to three things: the view, the water, and the sense of being somewhere genuinely restorative rather than merely photogenic.

## A Lake Shaped by Legend and a Resort Built on Imperial Ground

Lake Ashinoko's history is inseparable from Japanese spiritual and political life. Long before it became a resort destination, the lake was regarded as sacred, home to Hakone Shrine, whose iconic torii gate rises directly from the water near Motohakone. Local legend tells of a nine-headed dragon that once terrorized the lake before being subdued and transformed into a guardian deity — a story still honored each September during the Hakone Shrine Lake Festival, when boats process across the water in ceremonial tribute. This mythology is not incidental set dressing; it explains why the lakeshore has been treated as a place of reverence for well over a millennium, and why development around it has always been comparatively restrained.

The land on which The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko now stands carries its own historical weight. It was once part of the Hakone Detached Palace, an imperial villa established in the Meiji era as a retreat for the Japanese imperial family, chosen specifically for its cool mountain air and lake views during Tokyo's humid summers. Traces of that imperial history remain in the adjacent garden, officially preserved as a public park, where visitors can walk the same grounds once reserved exclusively for the court. When the Prince Hotels group developed the resort in the mid-twentieth century, it inherited not just the physical setting but the symbolic weight of a location once considered fit for royalty.

Hakone itself has functioned as a resting point on the old Tokaido road connecting Edo (Tokyo) and Kyoto since the Edo period, when travelers were required to pass through a strictly guarded checkpoint here, the remains of which are preserved a short distance from the hotel. This layered history — sacred lake, imperial retreat, feudal checkpoint — gives a stay at The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko a depth that purely modern resorts cannot replicate. Guests are not simply visiting a hotel; they are passing through a landscape that has been considered worth protecting and worth visiting for centuries.

## Getting There, Getting Settled, and Making the Most of Your Stay

Reaching The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko is straightforward, though a little planning improves the experience considerably. From Tokyo, the most common route is the Odakyu Romancecar from Shinjuku Station directly to Hakone-Yumoto Station, a journey of roughly 85 minutes. From there, travelers typically continue by the Hakone Tozan Bus, which runs a direct line to the hotel, or combine the Hakone Ropeway and Ashinoko Sightseeing Cruise for a scenic multi-stage arrival that turns the transfer itself into part of the trip. For those without heavy luggage constraints, purchasing the Hakone Freepass in advance is worth doing, since it covers most of the local trains, buses, ropeway, and pirate-style sightseeing ships used to explore the lake area, and pays for itself within a day of activity.

Booking strategy matters here more than at a typical city hotel. Rooms facing Mount Fuji sell out first, particularly in the winter months of December through February when the air is clearest and the mountain appears most vividly across the water; reserving three to four months ahead for a Fuji-view room is a reasonable rule of thumb. Travelers prioritizing the onsen experience over the view should instead focus on room categories closer to the bathing facilities, since the resort's hot spring baths draw on genuine volcanic-heated mineral water piped from the surrounding Hakone geothermal field, distinct from a simple heated pool.

Within the resort, a few practical habits make a real difference. Arrive at the outdoor rotenburo bath in the late afternoon before dinner, when the crowd is thinner and the light over the lake begins to soften — this is consistently the best window for photographs as well as quiet soaking. Meals are typically offered in both traditional kaiseki and international formats; opting for kaiseki at least once is worthwhile, as the seasonal menu draws on Hakone's mountain vegetables, Suruga Bay seafood, and Odawara-raised beef in a way that reflects the immediate region rather than a generic hotel banquet. Finally, guests wanting boat access to the lake should check morning departure times for the Ashinoko cruise ships, since the earliest sailings offer the calmest water and the best chance of an unobstructed Fuji reflection before wind picks up midday.

## Insider Perspectives for a More Rewarding Visit

Travel specialists who cover Hakone repeatedly tend to agree on a handful of points that don't always make it into standard listings. First, timing the visit around weather rather than season alone yields better Fuji views than chasing a particular month; late autumn mornings after a clear, dry night frequently outperform even the supposedly "best" winter months, so checking a short-range forecast before locking in dates is worth the effort if flexibility allows.

Second, pairing the stay with the Hakone Open-Air Museum or the Okada Museum of Art, both within a short drive, rounds out a trip that might otherwise be entirely nature-focused; Hakone's identity as both a hot spring town and an unexpectedly serious art destination is part of what distinguishes it from other onsen regions near Tokyo. Third, experienced guests recommend requesting a room on a higher floor of the main tower specifically for sunrise viewing, since the angle across the lake toward Fuji is markedly better than from ground-floor or garden-side rooms, even when those rooms are advertised as lake-facing.

Finally, for travelers combining The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko with a broader Hakone itinerary, building in an extra half-day for the Hakone Ropeway and the volcanic valley of Owakudani is consistently recommended, since rushing this segment is the most common regret reported by return visitors. The resort works best as an anchor for two or three nights rather than a single overnight stop, allowing enough time to experience both the property's own grounds and the wider Hakone area without feeling rushed.

## Conclusion: Why This Lakeside Stay Endures

The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko succeeds because it does not ask visitors to choose between history, nature, and comfort — it offers all three on the same stretch of shoreline. A lake once considered sacred, grounds once reserved for imperial retreat, and a modern resort built with genuine attention to onsen tradition and seasonal cuisine combine into a destination that rewards both first-time visitors and those returning for a third or fourth stay. For anyone planning time in Hakone, The Prince Hakone Lake Ashinoko remains one of the clearest examples of how a well-situated resort can turn a scenic lake into a complete, memorable journey.
