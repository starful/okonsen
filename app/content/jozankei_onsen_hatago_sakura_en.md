Stepping off the winding mountain road into Jozankei, the air changes almost immediately — cooler, damp with river mist, and faintly sulfuric in the most pleasant way. Hatago Sakura sits at the edge of this transformation, a modestly sized ryokan wedged between cedar-covered slopes and the churning green water of the Toyohira River. Unlike the sprawling resort hotels that dominate much of Jozankei's main strip, Sakura leans into intimacy. The lobby is small, warmly lit, and scented faintly with hinoki wood. Staff greet guests by name within minutes of check-in, a detail that surprises first-time visitors expecting the anonymity of a larger hot spring town property. What makes Sakura genuinely unique is its position: close enough to Sapporo to reach in under an hour, yet secluded enough that the sound of the river through your window completely replaces any trace of urban noise.

## A Hot Spring Town Born from a Herbalist's Discovery

Jozankei's onsen history dates back to 1866, when the Buddhist monk Miizumi Jozan first developed the springs after being guided to the area, reportedly following tips from local Ainu communities who had long known of the healing waters along the Toyohira gorge. The town that grew around this discovery became known as "Sapporo's Backyard Onsen" for its proximity to the prefectural capital. Hatago Sakura itself is a newer arrival relative to the town's oldest inns, built in the postwar decades as smaller family-run lodgings began filling the spaces between the grand hotels. Its name, meaning "cherry blossom," reflects a row of aging sakura trees planted along the inn's riverside path decades ago by a previous owner — trees that today form one of the more photographed private hanami spots in the region each May.

### Ainu Influence on the Region

Visitors interested in the area's deeper cultural roots will find subtle nods throughout Sakura's common areas, including carved wooden motifs echoing traditional Ainu patterns, a quiet acknowledgment of the indigenous heritage tied to these waters long before Japanese settlement formalized the onsen town.

## Waters Drawn from the Gorge: Mineral Composition and Views

The onsen at Hatago Sakura draws from Jozankei's characteristic **sodium chloride and calcium chloride springs**, classified as a weak salt spring (jun-ensen). The water carries a faint natural salinity that leaves skin notably smooth after bathing, and it's prized locally for retaining heat well, meaning bathers stay warm for hours after leaving the bath — a genuine advantage during Hokkaido's long, cold winters. Water temperature at the source runs around 70-80°C before being tempered for the baths, which typically sit between 41-43°C.

### The Riverside Rotenburo

The inn's signature open-air bath is built directly into the rock face overlooking the Toyohira River gorge. Steam rises off the water's surface and mingles with mist from the rapids below, and in autumn the surrounding maple canopy turns a deep crimson that reflects directly onto the bath's surface. In winter, snow accumulates on the black pine branches overhead while the water itself remains steaming — a classic **yukimi-buro** (snow-viewing bath) experience that draws repeat guests specifically for this season.

### Indoor Bath and Private Kashikiri Options

The indoor bath features floor-to-ceiling windows facing the same gorge, finished in local stone with a lower, more contemplative lighting scheme suited to late-night or early-morning soaks. Sakura also offers a reservable private family bath (kashikiri-buro), a smaller stone-lined pool ideal for couples, families with small children, or guests with tattoos who prefer to avoid the communal baths entirely.

## Wabi-Sabi Simplicity Meets Riverside Comfort

Rooms at Hatago Sakura favor understated Japanese aesthetics over ornamentation. Tatami flooring, shoji screens, and low wooden furniture define the majority of the twenty-some guest rooms, many of which face the river directly. The architecture leans into **wabi-sabi** principles: exposed wood grain left untreated, subtly irregular plaster walls, and an overall palette of muted browns, greens, and off-whites that draws the eye outward to the forest rather than inward to decoration. Several of the higher-tier rooms include their own private open-air bath on a small balcony overlooking the water, a popular upgrade for anniversary trips. Futons are laid out each evening by staff while guests are at dinner, following traditional ryokan service, and rooms are equipped with modern touches — climate control, quality linens, and reliably fast Wi-Fi — discreetly integrated so as not to disrupt the visual calm.

## Kaiseki Dining Rooted in Hokkaido's Terroir

Dinner at Sakura is a multi-course kaiseki meal built almost entirely around Hokkaido's exceptional local produce, served either in-room or in a private dining alcove depending on room grade.

### Dinner Highlights

The meal typically opens with a seasonal **sakizuke** amuse-bouche — perhaps a small dish of sea urchin and mountain vegetable in a light vinegar dressing — followed by sashimi featuring Hokkaido staples like scallop, salmon, and seasonal white fish sourced from nearby Otaru or Ishikari Bay. A standout course is often the **Ishikari nabe**, a salmon and vegetable hot pot simmered tableside in a miso-based broth, a dish with deep roots in Hokkaido home cooking. Grilled courses frequently showcase Jingisukan-style Hokkaido lamb or a rich cut of local Hokkaido wagyu, seared over binchotan charcoal. The meal closes with a delicate chawanmushi, a rice course using Hokkaido's famed short-grain rice, and a seasonal dessert such as melon or Hokkaido milk pudding.

### Breakfast

Breakfast follows a traditional washoku format: grilled fish, simmered vegetables, house-made tofu, miso soup, rice, and pickles, with an emphasis on local dairy — Hokkaido's yogurt and butter frequently make appearances alongside the standard spread.

## Beyond the Baths: Exploring Jozankei and Sapporo

Jozankei's compact town center is walkable from Sakura and includes small craft shops, a riverside footpath dotted with public foot baths (ashiyu), and access to the **Toyohira River gorge trails**, popular for short hikes with dramatic rock formations. Nearby, the **Jozankei Dam** and **Hoheikyo Onsen** area offer additional scenic detours for guests with a car or willing to take a local bus. Because Sapporo's city center is roughly 50-60 minutes away by car or bus, many guests combine a Sakura stay with a day trip into the city for Sapporo's food markets, the Sapporo Beer Museum, or Odori Park, particularly convenient during the Sapporo Snow Festival in February when the ryokan makes an excellent quieter alternative to staying downtown.

## Booking Smart: Tattoos, Seasons, and Timing

Hatago Sakura follows the increasingly common flexible policy among smaller Hokkaido ryokan: visible tattoos are generally accepted in the mixed-gender or time-slotted baths, though guests with larger tattoos may be asked to use the private kashikiri bath as a courtesy to other guests — it's worth confirming directly when booking. The best seasons to visit are **autumn (late September through October)** for the maple foliage along the gorge, and **winter (December through February)** for the snow-viewing baths, though this is also peak demand alongside the Sapporo Snow Festival, so book at least two months ahead for winter weekends. Spring brings the inn's namesake cherry blossoms into bloom typically in early May, slightly later than mainland Japan due to Hokkaido's climate. Weekday stays offer noticeably better rates and availability than weekends, and booking directly through the ryokan's own reservation line sometimes unlocks small perks like a room upgrade or early check-in not available through third-party sites.

## Getting There from Sapporo

The most common route from New Chitose Airport or central Sapporo is by car, taking roughly 60-90 minutes via Route 230 into the mountains. For guests without a car, a direct bus service (the Jozankei Onsen line) runs from Sapporo Station and Makomanai Station, taking approximately 60-70 minutes, with Sakura providing guidance on the nearest stop and a short walk or shuttle arrangement into the ryokan itself. Taxis from central Sapporo are also an option for a more direct, if pricier, transfer of about 50 minutes.
