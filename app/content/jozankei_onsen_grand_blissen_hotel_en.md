Arriving in Jozankei after the winding forty-minute drive from central Sapporo feels like passing through an invisible membrane. One moment you're navigating city traffic; the next, the Toyohira River gorge opens up around you, walled in by cedar and maple, with plumes of geothermal steam rising from vents along the roadside. Grand Blissen Hotel sits directly on this river bend, and the first impression on checking in is one of scale softened by warmth — a soaring lobby of blond hardwood and volcanic stone, the low murmur of running water audible even before you reach the baths. Staff greet you not with stiff formality but with the easy hospitality typical of Hokkaido's tourism culture, quick with local recommendations delivered in fluent, friendly English. What distinguishes Grand Blissen from the dozens of other properties lining this valley is its unusual doubling as both onsen retreat and gateway lodge: close enough to Sapporo for a spontaneous overnight trip, remote enough that by nightfall you'll hear nothing but the river and the wind through the ravine.

## The Gorge That Made a Spa Town

Jozankei's history begins not with nobility or shoguns but with a Buddhist monk. In 1866, the priest Miizumi Jozan trekked into this then-unmapped ravine while searching for a route into the Hokkaido interior, and stumbled upon hot water bubbling directly from the riverbed. He is credited with opening the area to bathers, and the town itself borrows his name — Jozankei, "Jozan's Gorge." What began as a rustic bathhouse for exhausted travelers and loggers evolved, over the following century, into Sapporo's designated *okuzashiki*, or "inner parlor" — a retreat where the city's residents and visiting dignitaries would unwind after business in the capital. Grand Blissen itself is a newer arrival to this lineage, built during the resort's late-20th-century expansion when larger, amenity-rich hotels began replacing the original wooden inns. Yet the property has been careful to honor Jozankei's founding identity: framed photographs of the gorge's Meiji-era bathhouses line the corridor near the lobby, and the hotel's landscaping deliberately echoes the wild, unmanicured cedar forest that first drew Jozan here.

## Water From the Volcano's Edge

The onsen water at Grand Blissen is classified as a sodium-calcium chloride spring, sourced from geothermal activity tied to the nearby volcanic zone around Mount Muine and the Shikotsu-Toya volcanic belt. It emerges from the source at a scalding temperature and is cooled to a bathing range of around 40–42°C before reaching the tubs. Chemically, this is a "warming" water — its salt content coats the skin in a thin mineral film that slows heat loss, meaning bathers stay warm for hours after leaving the tub, a quality especially prized during Hokkaido's brutal winters. The water carries a faint, not unpleasant salinity and is prized locally for easing muscle fatigue, chronic joint pain, and poor circulation.

### The Canyon-View Rotenburo

The hotel's signature open-air bath is built directly into the cliffside overlooking the Toyohira River gorge, angled so that bathers face the water rather than the hotel itself. In autumn, the opposing cliff face erupts into a wall of crimson and gold maple, reflected in the churning river below — arguably one of the most photogenic onsen views in Hokkaido, though guests are of course asked to leave cameras behind at the changing room. In winter, thick snow accumulates on the rock ledges surrounding the bath, creating a dramatic contrast between the steaming black water and the white gorge walls, with occasional sightings of Japanese macaques traversing the far bank.

### Indoor Baths and the Ganban-yoku Stone Sauna

Beyond the rotenburo, Grand Blissen operates a large indoor bathing hall with floor-to-ceiling windows facing the gorge, plus a *ganban-yoku* (heated stone bed sauna) using volcanic rock warmed from beneath — a gentler, low-humidity alternative to a traditional sauna, popular with guests seeking detoxification without the intensity of steam. Private family baths (*kashikiri-buro*) can be reserved by the hour, ideal for couples, families with small children, or tattooed guests who prefer not to use the communal facilities.

## Where Wood Meets Wabi-Sabi

Grand Blissen's guest rooms split roughly into three categories: traditional tatami rooms, modern Western-style twins, and hybrid rooms that combine a raised tatami sleeping area with Western furniture in an adjoining sitting space. The design philosophy across all categories leans into an understated, textural minimalism — exposed cedar beams, washi-paper lighting fixtures that cast a soft amber glow, and tokonoma alcoves displaying a single seasonal ikebana arrangement rather than ornate decoration. Higher-tier rooms include their own semi-open-air bath on a private balcony, plumbed directly with the same onsen water as the communal baths, allowing guests to soak privately while overlooking the ravine at any hour. Beds in Western-style rooms use plush, deep mattresses that lean toward Western comfort standards rather than firmer traditional futon, a detail international guests consistently appreciate. Amenities are generously stocked, including yukata in multiple sizes, hanten jackets for cooler evenings, and high-end skincare products from Hokkaido-based botanical brands.

## A Kaiseki Built on Hokkaido's Harvest

Dinner at Grand Blissen is a multi-course kaiseki meal built almost entirely around Hokkaido's exceptional local produce — a real point of pride for the kitchen, which changes its menu seasonally to track the island's fishing and harvest calendars. A typical autumn course might open with a *sakizuke* amuse-bouche of chilled uni custard, followed by *zensai* assortments featuring Ishikari salmon roe, Hokkaido snow crab, and pickled mountain vegetables foraged from the surrounding hills. The *owan* soup course frequently spotlights matsutake mushroom in a clear dashi broth, prized for its earthy, almost cinnamon-like aroma. A sashimi course showcases the day's catch from the Sea of Japan, often including scallop, sea urchin, and fatty salmon, while the centerpiece grilled course rotates between Hokkaido wagyu, locally raised Genghis Khan lamb (a Hokkaido specialty, though usually served as a smaller tasting portion here rather than the full barbecue format), or charcoal-grilled king crab legs. Rice is finished with seasonal additions like corn or chestnut, both iconic Hokkaido autumn crops, and dessert typically features Yubari melon or a Hokkaido dairy-based panna cotta, leaning on the region's famously rich milk. Breakfast the next morning is a generous buffet-and-set hybrid, pairing grilled fish, house-made tofu, and rice with regional specialties like ikura don stations and fresh-baked bread from Sapporo bakeries — a nod to Hokkaido's own hybrid culinary identity.

## Beyond the Gorge: Sapporo and Beyond

Guests based at Grand Blissen have an unusually strong home base for exploring greater Sapporo. The gorge itself offers riverside walking trails, the Jozankei Dam observation area, and — depending on season — access to nearby ski fields including Sapporo Kokusai, roughly a 30-minute drive away, making Grand Blissen a legitimate ski-and-soak base in winter. Closer to town, the Toyohira River's *Nikoniko Gate* wooden statues, a quirky local landmark, are worth a quick photo stop. For those willing to venture into central Sapporo, the Sapporo Beer Museum, Odori Park, and the historic Former Hokkaido Government Office Building are all within striking distance, and the city's famous Susukino district offers a stark contrast of neon-lit nightlife to the onsen town's tranquility. History-minded travelers should also consider the Historical Village of Hokkaido, an open-air museum of preserved Meiji and Taisho-era buildings roughly 40 minutes away, which contextualizes the same pioneer-era history that shaped Jozankei's founding.

## Planning Your Soak

Grand Blissen operates a tattoo-friendly policy for private family baths but maintains standard restrictions in communal areas — travelers with visible tattoos should reserve a *kashikiri-buro* slot at check-in, as these fill quickly on weekends. The best seasons for a visit split sharply by aesthetic preference: October offers the valley's celebrated autumn foliage, drawing the heaviest crowds and requiring bookings well in advance, while January through February delivers deep snow, dramatically quieter baths, and the surreal experience of soaking in an open-air rotenburo as snowflakes fall. Booking directly through the hotel's own site rather than third-party aggregators frequently unlocks better room-upgrade availability and early check-in, and midweek stays (Sunday through Thursday) tend to run noticeably cheaper than weekend rates.

## Getting to the Gorge

The most straightforward access route is by car or taxi from New Chitose Airport or central Sapporo, roughly 60–90 minutes depending on traffic and starting point. Travelers without a car can take the Jotetsu Bus directly from Sapporo Station or Makomanai Subway Station, with dedicated Jozankei-bound lines running frequently throughout the day; the ride takes approximately 60–70 minutes and drops passengers within a short walk of the hotel. Many guests also opt for the hotel's own shuttle service, which should be reserved in advance, or a private transfer arranged through the concierge — a comfortable, hassle-free option after a long international flight into New Chitose.
