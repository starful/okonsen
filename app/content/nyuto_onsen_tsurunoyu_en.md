## Introduction

There are onsens, and then there is **Tsurunoyu**. Nestled at the end of a narrow, winding forest road in Nyuto Onsen village, deep in the Ou Mountains of Akita Prefecture, this ryokan exists in a category entirely its own. The thatched main building (*honkan*) that greets arriving guests is not a reproduction of Edo-period design — it *is* an Edo-period building, one that has sheltered weary travelers and regional lords for over three centuries. The sight of that steep, snow-laden thatch roof, lanterns glowing warmly in the half-light, framed by towering cryptomeria trees and the gentle sound of rushing mountain water, is one of the most quietly astonishing first impressions in Japanese travel.

Tsurunoyu — whose name translates to "crane's bath" — is the oldest and arguably most revered of the seven ryokans that make up Nyuto Onsen village (*onsen-kyo*). The village sits at roughly 750 meters above sea level, fully immersed in the Towada-Hachimantai National Park, and the road in feels deliberately slow — as though the mountain itself is asking you to decompress before you arrive. In winter, the transformation is absolute: snow smothers every surface, steam rises from the baths into frigid air, and the world shrinks to the radius of firelight. It is this combination of historical authenticity, spectacular natural setting, and genuinely restorative spring water that makes Tsurunoyu not merely popular but **legendary**.

## History & Heritage

Tsurunoyu's origins trace back to the early Edo period, when the *bushi* (samurai) class of the Akita domain — specifically the Satake clan who held the region — are said to have used the springs as a private bathing retreat and hunting lodge. The property's written records date to around 1659, making it one of Japan's oldest continuously operating onsen inns. The thatched-roof *honkan*, built in a style known as *kabuto-zukuri* (helmet-shaped roof construction), has been carefully maintained and partially rebuilt over the centuries while preserving its foundational form and spirit.

Unlike many historic ryokans that have modernized their facilities into contemporary luxury, Tsurunoyu has resisted wholesale renovation. Guest rooms in the *honkan* retain wood-burning stoves (*kamadon*), original wooden floors worn smooth by generations of *yukata*-clad guests, and a genuine absence of televisions in many rooms. This is a deliberate preservation of character, not neglect — and it attracts guests who understand the difference. The ryokan has been recognized by Japanese architectural and cultural preservation bodies, and it regularly appears in lists of Japan's most atmospheric inns.

The surrounding Nyuto Onsen village grew around the springs over subsequent centuries, with additional inns established to serve an increasing flow of travelers seeking the therapeutic waters. Today the seven ryokans of Nyuto Onsen coexist harmoniously, connected by a shared *nyuto-kyo passbook* that allows guests staying at any property to visit the baths of the others — a tradition that encourages exploration of the varied water types found across the valley.

## Baths & Spring Water

Tsurunoyu draws from **multiple spring sources**, each with distinct chemical compositions and visual properties, and this is the ryokan's central attraction. The most famous is the milky-white, sulfurous *nigori-yu* (cloudy water) — an opaque, slightly turbid liquid that turns white upon contact with air due to its high hydrogen sulfide content. Soaking in this water is a tactile experience unlike any other; the liquid feels remarkably soft and enveloping, and the gentle sulfurous scent, which might seem strong at first, becomes deeply relaxing as the body adjusts.

The main outdoor communal bath is the **Konyoku Rotenburo** (混浴露天風呂), and it is one of the most photographed onsen settings in Japan. A large, irregularly shaped pool of milky-white water extends into the open air, edged by natural stone and backed by the green or white forest depending on the season. It is mixed-gender (*konyoku*), meaning men and women bathe together — a practice rooted in centuries of Japanese onsen tradition rather than modern liberalism. Women wishing to enter the konyoku bath while maintaining modesty are welcome to wear a *bathing cloth* (*yu-maki*), which can be purchased or borrowed at the front desk.

Beyond the konyoku, Tsurunoyu operates several **women-only** outdoor and indoor baths, ensuring that all guests have access to the spring water in an environment where they feel entirely comfortable. There is also a **men's indoor bath** and a selection of **private family baths** (*kashikiri*) that can be reserved by the hour for an additional fee — ideal for couples or guests who prefer complete privacy.

The waters are said to be effective for easing neuralgia, muscle fatigue, joint pain, and skin conditions. Guests frequently describe the sensation after bathing as a warm, slow-releasing relaxation that lasts well into the night.

**Tattoo policy**: The konyoku and communal indoor baths at Tsurunoyu follow traditional Japanese onsen conventions, which typically do not permit visible tattoos. Guests with tattoos should use the private *kashikiri* baths, which can be booked specifically for this purpose, ensuring a comfortable and enjoyable stay.

## Rooms & Accommodation

Tsurunoyu offers accommodations across several building types, ranging from the atmospheric *honkan* (main building) rooms to newer annexes (*bekkan*). **Honkan rooms** are the most sought-after and must be booked well in advance. These rooms retain original wooden construction, low ceilings, *shoji* screen windows that look out onto the forest or inner garden, and in many cases a wood-burning stove (*teppan kamadon*) — a necessity during the long Akita winters. Futon bedding is prepared on tatami floors each evening by staff. The atmosphere is dense with authenticity.

The newer annexe buildings offer somewhat more spacious rooms with modern comforts, though they maintain a traditional aesthetic. All room categories include *yukata*, towels, and basic toiletries. Guests staying overnight receive unlimited access to all bath facilities throughout their stay, including both the konyoku and women-only pools.

It is important to understand that Tsurunoyu's appeal is rooted in its **simplicity and immersion in nature**, not in luxury amenities. There is no spa, no pool, no karaoke room. The attraction is the bath, the forest, the seasons, and the silence. Guests who align with this philosophy invariably describe their stay as one of the most memorable experiences of their Japanese travels.

## Dining

Dinner at Tsurunoyu is a seasonal *kaiseki* course, served either in-room or in a communal dining space depending on the room type. The kitchen sources heavily from Akita Prefecture's exceptional larder — a region celebrated throughout Japan for the quality of its rice, mountain vegetables (*sansai*), freshwater fish, and *sake*.

A typical dinner course might open with fern shoots (*zenmai*) and *warabi* fiddleheads dressed in *sesame miso*, proceed through sashimi of river fish, *tofu* made from locally grown soybeans, and miso soup with foraged mushrooms, before arriving at the signature dish: Akita *hinai-jidori* chicken — a prized heritage breed known for its rich, deep flavor — prepared as a slow-simmered *kiritampo nabe* (rice-cylinder hot pot). This dish, specific to Akita, is warming and deeply satisfying, particularly after an evening bath in cold weather. Rice is served from local Akita *koshihikari* varieties, often considered among Japan's finest.

Breakfast follows a traditional Japanese format: grilled fish, pickled vegetables, *tofu*, *natto* (fermented soybeans), steamed rice, and *miso* soup. The simplicity is perfect — it feels nourishing rather than performative, calibrated to guests who plan to spend the morning in the baths before departing or exploring the village.

## Local Area

Nyuto Onsen village itself is the primary attraction of the surrounding area, and a **Nyuto Onsenkyo Tegata** (湯めぐり帖) passbook allows guests staying at any of the seven member ryokans — Tsurunoyu, Kuroyu, Magoroku, Oonarudake, Ogama, Tajikara, and Ganiba — to visit the baths of the others at no additional charge. Each ryokan has distinct water types and bath environments, making a morning or afternoon of "bath hopping" through the valley a rewarding and deeply enjoyable way to spend a day.

A short distance from the village, **Lake Tazawa** (*Tazawako*) is the deepest lake in Japan, renowned for its intense cobalt-blue color that persists year-round due to the lake's exceptional purity and depth. The lake is accessible by rental bike or car from Tazawako Station and makes an excellent half-day excursion, particularly in autumn when the surrounding foliage turns vivid red and orange.

Further east, **Kakunodate** is a beautifully preserved *samurai town* with several intact *buke-yashiki* (samurai residences) and some of Japan's most celebrated weeping cherry trees (*shidare-zakura*), making it an ideal cultural contrast to the mountain quiet of Nyuto Onsen — best visited as a day trip from the ryokan.

In winter, the Nyuto area is also within reach of **Tazawako Ski Resort**, located at the base of Mt. Akita Komagatake, offering varied terrain for skiers and snowboarders.

## Practical Tips

**Reservations**: Tsurunoyu is one of Japan's most popular historic ryokans and rooms — especially *honkan* rooms — book out months in advance, particularly for autumn foliage season (late October–mid November), New Year, and winter weekends. Reservations via the official website or phone are essential; same-day or walk-in stays for overnight accommodation are rarely possible.

**Day-trip bathing**: Tsurunoyu does accept day visitors (*higaeri nyūyoku*) for bath use only, typically in the morning hours. However, policies and availability can change seasonally; confirm directly before visiting.

**What to bring**: Tsurunoyu's basic philosophy means you should come prepared: warm layers for cool evenings, comfortable footwear for the uneven stone paths, and a small toiletry bag for the baths. The ryokan provides *yukata*, *geta*, and basic bath amenities.

**Konyoku etiquette**: Approach the mixed-gender bath with the same quiet, unhurried demeanor you would any Japanese onsen. Wash thoroughly before entering. Minimal conversation, phones strictly away, and respectful awareness of other bathers are all non-negotiable.

**Season considerations**: Each season at Nyuto is exceptional. Spring brings snow melt and fresh mountain air; summer offers lush green forest; autumn is ablaze with color; winter is the most photogenic and most atmospheric. Book for the season that calls to you, but be aware that winter access can be affected by road conditions.

## Access

Tsurunoyu is intentionally remote, and reaching it is part of the experience.

**By Train and Bus**:
The nearest Shinkansen station is **Tazawako Station** on the Akita Shinkansen line (direct from Tokyo in approximately 2 hours 45 minutes to 3 hours). From Tazawako Station, take the **Ushitabi Bus** (うし滝バス) bound for Nyuto Onsen. The bus runs several times daily and takes approximately 50 minutes to reach the Tsurunoyu-mae stop. Schedules are seasonal — check the Ugo Kotsu bus timetable before travel, and note that service is reduced outside peak periods.

**By Car**:
From Tazawako Station, the drive to Tsurunoyu takes approximately 30–40 minutes via Route 46 and the mountain access road. The final section of the road is narrow and winding; in winter it requires snow tires and caution. The ryokan has a designated parking area.

**Shuttle**: Tsurunoyu offers a shuttle service from Tazawako Station for overnight guests on specific days and schedules. Confirm availability and timing when making your reservation.

The journey into the mountains — whether by bus threading through cedar forest or by car along the river — acts as a natural decompression: by the time you arrive, the steam rising from the baths feels like a welcome you've been moving toward all day.
