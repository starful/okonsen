There are onsen ryokans, and then there is Nyuto Onsen Magoroku — a place that feels less like a hotel and more like a portal into a forgotten Japan. Nestled at the end of a winding mountain road inside Towada-Hachimantai National Park, Magoroku greets arriving guests with the sight of a steep, moss-flecked thatched roof rising above cedar and beech trees. The air smells faintly of sulfur and woodsmoke. There is no glass lobby, no polished marble — just a low wooden entrance, worn stone steps, and the quiet hum of a mountain stream nearby. It is easy to understand within minutes why this ryokan, part of the legendary Nyuto Onsen-kyo cluster, has become a bucket-list destination for onsen purists from around the world.

## A Living Relic of Edo-Era Bathing Culture

Magoroku's history stretches back over three centuries, with records suggesting the ryokan has operated in some form since the mid-1600s, making it one of the oldest continuously run hot spring inns in the Tohoku region. The name "Nyuto" (乳頭), meaning "nipple," is said to derive from the shape of nearby Nyuto-san mountain, and the entire onsen village was historically used as a *toji* — a therapeutic long-stay retreat where farmers and travelers came to heal injuries and fatigue after the harvest season. Magoroku itself is believed to have originally served as a lodging house for samurai and pilgrims traveling the old Kunimi Pass trade route. Today, the property still bears the marks of that lineage: hand-hewn beams blackened by decades of irori hearth smoke, sliding paper doors softened with age, and floorboards that creak with the same rhythm they likely did generations ago. Unlike many onsen towns that modernized rapidly during the postwar boom, Nyuto Onsen — and Magoroku in particular — deliberately preserved its Edo-period character, resisting large-scale renovation in favor of authenticity.

## The Milky White Waters: Magoroku's Crown Jewel

The defining feature of Magoroku is undoubtedly its outdoor bath, the Kokuyu-no-Yu, often cited as one of the most photographed rotenburo in all of Japan. The water here is a cloudy, opalescent white — a striking contrast to the clear or amber-toned springs found elsewhere in the region. This coloration comes from a high concentration of sulfur and metasilicic acid, which scatters light as it passes through suspended mineral particles. The spring is classified as a simple sulfur spring (単純硫黄泉), slightly acidic, and prized for its purported benefits for skin conditions, joint pain, and circulatory issues. Bathers soak while gazing directly at a dense stand of Japanese beech trees, with the sound of the Senjo-numa stream trickling just beyond the rocks. In autumn, the surrounding canopy turns fiery red and gold; in winter, steam rises dramatically against a backdrop of snow-laden branches, creating what many consider the single most iconic onsen scene in the country. Notably, this particular bath has historically operated as **konyoku** (mixed-gender bathing), a tradition dating back centuries, though women-only hours and private options are also available for those seeking more privacy. Indoor baths, fed by a separate source, offer a milder, more relaxed soak year-round, with large wooden-framed windows overlooking the forest.

### Water Composition Details

The mineral content is notably rich in sulfate and sulfide ions, giving the water its faint egg-like scent — a hallmark of genuine sulfur springs. Unlike heavily circulated urban baths, Magoroku's water is drawn directly from a natural source and flows continuously (kake-nagashi), meaning what touches your skin has traveled straight from deep underground without chemical treatment or recirculation.

## Timeworn Wood and Wabi-Sabi Interiors

Magoroku's guest rooms embody a philosophy of intentional imperfection. Most rooms feature simple tatami flooring, low wooden tables, and futon bedding laid out each evening by staff — a ritual that itself feels like a small ceremony. There is no attempt to disguise the age of the building; exposed beams, hand-planed wood grain, and slightly uneven walls are treated as features rather than flaws. Some rooms retain original Meiji- and Taisho-era fixtures, while newer annex buildings offer slightly more updated comforts, including private toilets, for travelers who want the atmosphere without sacrificing all modern convenience. The heart of the main building is its irori sunken hearth lounge, where guests can sit on tatami cushions around a smoldering charcoal fire — a communal space that has functioned as the social center of the ryokan for generations. Corridors are narrow and dimly lit with paper lanterns, intentionally encouraging a slower, quieter pace of movement through the building.

## Kaiseki Dining Rooted in the Mountain Harvest

Dinner at Magoroku is a showcase of Akita's mountain and river bounty, served in a multi-course kaiseki format either in your room or a communal dining hall, depending on the season and plan. Expect dishes built around **sansai** (wild mountain vegetables) such as fiddlehead ferns, bamboo shoots, and mountain yam, foraged from the surrounding hills. Iwana (mountain trout) and yamame are frequently grilled whole over charcoal, salted simply to let the freshness shine. Akita's famous **kiritanpo** — pounded rice formed around a skewer and roasted — often appears in a savory hot pot alongside free-range chicken and burdock root. Seasonal specialties rotate throughout the year: matsutake mushroom dishes in autumn, wild boar hot pot in winter, and delicate spring vegetables in the warmer months. Breakfast is a comparatively humble but deeply satisfying affair — grilled fish, house-made pickles, rice cooked over a wood fire, miso soup, and a soft-cooked egg, often enjoyed communally around the irori hearth as morning light filters through the wooden lattice windows.

## Exploring the Nyuto Onsen-kyo and Semboku Region

Magoroku sits at the heart of the Nyuto Onsen-kyo, a cluster of seven historic ryokans connected by forest trails, making onsen-hopping (with a shared "yumeguri" pass) one of the most popular activities for guests staying multiple nights. Hiking trails lead toward Nyuto-san and into Towada-Hachimantai National Park, with routes suited for both casual walkers and serious trekkers. A short drive away, Lake Tazawa — Japan's deepest lake, famous for its cobalt-blue water and the golden Tatsuko statue — offers scenic drives and lakeside dining. History and craft lovers should visit nearby Kakunodate, often called "Little Kyoto of Tohoku," known for its preserved samurai residences, cherry-tree-lined streets, and traditional cherry-bark craftwork (kabazaiku). In winter, the region transforms into a snow-country wonderland, with the nearby Tazawako Ski Resort offering some of Japan's best backcountry powder skiing.

## Tattoo Policy, Best Seasons, and Booking Advice

Given its historic mixed-gender bathing tradition and rural character, Magoroku's tattoo policy tends to be more relaxed than that of urban onsen resorts, though it is always wise to confirm current rules directly with the ryokan before arrival, as policies can shift. The best times to visit are early October for peak autumn foliage around the outdoor bath, and January through February for a dramatic snow-and-steam atmosphere — though winter access requires extra travel planning. Rooms book out many months in advance, especially for weekends and holiday periods, so reserving three to six months ahead is strongly advised. Because English-language booking platforms don't always list Magoroku directly, working through a Japanese-speaking travel agent or calling the ryokan directly can improve availability chances.

## Getting to Magoroku

The most common access route is via Tazawako Station on the Akita Shinkansen, roughly ninety minutes from Tokyo. From the station, a shuttle bus operated by Ugo Kotsu runs directly to Nyuto Onsen (approximately 50 minutes), with Magoroku typically requiring a short walk or transfer from the main stop, as it sits slightly apart from the central cluster along a forest path. Travelers driving from Tokyo or Sendai can reach the area via the Tohoku Expressway, exiting near Kakunodate before continuing on mountain roads toward Nyuto Onsen — a route that can be snow-affected in winter, so winter tires or a 4WD rental are essential between December and March.
