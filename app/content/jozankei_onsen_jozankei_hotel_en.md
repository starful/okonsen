## First Impressions: Sapporo's Hidden Gorge

Arriving at **Jozankei Hotel** feels like slipping through a seam in the city. Sapporo's grid of neon and department stores recedes within thirty minutes, replaced by a steep-walled river gorge cloaked in Ezo spruce and Mongolian oak. The Toyohira River churns turquoise-green below the property, a color locals attribute to dissolved minerals carried down from the volcanic peaks upstream. The hotel itself presents a modern, tiered facade that steps down the hillside toward the water, and the lobby's floor-to-ceiling windows frame the gorge like a living scroll painting. What distinguishes Jozankei Hotel from the smaller ryokan clustered along the main street is its scale and its unapologetic embrace of destination-resort comfort: broad public baths, multiple dining venues, and rooms engineered specifically to maximize river views. It is a place built for unwinding after a day of Sapporo sightseeing, not for austere ritual, and that accessibility is precisely its appeal.

## From Timber Camps to Therapeutic Retreat

Jozankei's onsen history traces back to 1866, when the Buddhist priest Miizumi Jozan, exiled from Honshu, discovered the hot springs while trekking through what was then uncharted Ezo wilderness. He is credited with opening the first bathhouse, and the town retains his name to this day. For decades, Jozankei served primarily loggers and surveyors working the Hokkaido frontier, offering respite from grueling manual labor in the cold. As Sapporo grew into Hokkaido's administrative capital after the 1972 Winter Olympics put the region on the international map, Jozankei transformed into the city's designated "backyard hot spring," a title it still carries proudly. Jozankei Hotel itself emerged from this mid-century boom, expanding over successive renovations into the large-format resort it is today, while still drawing on the same geothermal veins that sustained the original Meiji-era bathhouses.

## The Water: Sodium Chloride Springs Facing the Gorge

The onsen water at Jozankei Hotel is classified as a **sodium-calcium chloride spring** (weakly alkaline, hypotonic), drawn from sources feeding the entire valley. This mineral profile is prized for its heat-retention properties; the salt content coats the skin in a thin film that slows evaporative cooling, meaning bathers stay warm long after leaving the tub — a genuine advantage during Hokkaido's brutal winters. The water is also reputed locally as **"the hot spring of a thousand cures,"** traditionally associated with relief from neuralgia, muscle fatigue, and poor circulation. The hotel's signature bath is its expansive open-air rotenburo, cantilevered directly over the Toyohira gorge, where steam rises to meet the cold air and mixes with the sound of the rapids below. In winter, bathers soak chin-deep while snow accumulates silently on the rocks and pine boughs just beyond the bath's edge, a sensory contrast — scalding water, frigid air — that regulars describe as almost meditative. Indoor bathing halls offer large-format tiled tubs and panoramic windows for those visiting during the region's frequent rain, plus sauna and cold-plunge facilities for a rotation-style bathing routine increasingly popular among Hokkaido spa-goers.

### Seasonal Character of the Baths

The rotenburo transforms dramatically across the calendar. Spring brings a rush of snowmelt that swells the river into a louder, more energetic backdrop. Summer opens the gorge's full green canopy, with cicadas providing a dense wall of sound. Autumn ignites the surrounding maples and oaks into a corridor of red and gold visible directly from the tub. Winter, however, is when Jozankei is best known: heavy Hokkaido snowfall blankets the rocks and railings in white, and the bath becomes an exercise in contrast bathing that many consider the definitive Hokkaido onsen experience.

## Rooms Built Around the River View

Jozankei Hotel's guest rooms lean toward a clean, contemporary interpretation of Japanese hospitality rather than rustic wabi-sabi minimalism. Most rooms combine tatami-floored seating areas with low chabudai tables alongside Western-style beds, a hybrid layout suited to travelers unfamiliar with futon bedding. The defining feature across nearly every room category is orientation: units are arranged so that the tatami alcove or window seat faces directly onto the river gorge, allowing guests to watch the water change color with the light throughout the day. Higher-grade rooms include private open-air baths on balconies suspended above the ravine, fed by the same mineral-rich source as the communal facilities, giving guests a fully private soak without leaving their room. Interiors favor warm wood paneling, paper-shaded lighting, and restrained color palettes of charcoal, cream, and moss green, evoking the surrounding forest without resorting to overt rustic props. Corridors and public lounges are similarly wide and light-filled, designed for the elderly and family groups who make up a significant share of Jozankei's clientele.

## A Kaiseki Table Built on Hokkaido's Larder

Dinner at Jozankei Hotel is where the region's agricultural and marine wealth takes center stage. The kaiseki course typically opens with a sakizuke amuse-bouche — perhaps a single scallop dressed in yuzu vinegar — before moving into a sashimi course built around Hokkaido's cold-water specialties: fatty hokke, sweet botan ebi (spot prawns), and, in season, uni harvested from the nearby Sea of Japan coast. A hallmark of any Hokkaido kaiseki is the crab course, usually featuring king crab or snow crab served both raw and lightly steamed to showcase the different textures. A hot pot course follows, frequently built around Ishikari-nabe, a salmon and vegetable stew flavored with miso that originated in Hokkaido and pairs the region's iconic fish with root vegetables like potato and daikon grown in the volcanic soil nearby. Wagyu or Hokkaido-bred pork appears grilled tableside on a small hoba-leaf or stone plate, finished with a reduction of local miso or sansho pepper. Rice is typically Hokkaido-grown Yumepirika or Nanatsuboshi, prized nationally for its sheen and mild sweetness, served alongside a clear soup and pickles to close the meal. Breakfast shifts to a more restrained set menu: grilled salmon, a soft-cooked egg, nori, miso soup thickened with local vegetables, and small dishes of simmered mountain greens — fuel for a day of exploring Sapporo or the surrounding trails.

## Beyond the Baths: Sapporo and the Jozankei Valley

Jozankei's proximity to Sapporo is its greatest practical advantage. The Sapporo Beer Museum, Odori Park, and the Nijo Fish Market are all reachable within roughly forty-five minutes by car or shuttle bus, making a day trip into the city entirely feasible without repacking bags. Closer to the hotel, the Jozankei Dam observation area and the Toyohira River gorge trails offer short hikes with dramatic viewpoints, particularly vivid during autumn foliage season in early-to-mid October. Winter visitors can reach several ski resorts, including Sapporo Kokusai, within a thirty-minute drive, pairing daytime powder runs with evening onsen recovery. The town of Jozankei itself hosts a small but charming footbath (ashiyu) strip along the river, along with local craft shops selling Ainu-inspired woodwork, and the Jozankei Farm, a rest-stop offering local dairy and produce popular with families. In summer, the annual Jozankei Yukata Matsuri fills the streets with lantern-lit parades, a good excuse to time a visit around early August.

## Practical Wisdom Before You Book

Regarding tattoos: like most large Japanese hotel-style onsen, Jozankei Hotel maintains a **no-tattoo policy** in its communal baths, though private in-room baths bypass this restriction entirely, making them the easiest workaround for tattooed travelers. Snow-covered winter (December through February) delivers the most photogenic rotenburo experience but requires cautious footing on icy paths — the hotel provides non-slip footwear at bath entrances. For crowd avoidance, weekday stays sidestep the domestic tour groups that frequently book Jozankei on weekends, particularly during Golden Week and Obon. Booking a river-facing room with a private bath well in advance is recommended for anniversary or honeymoon travel, as these units sell out fastest. Complimentary shuttle buses run between the hotel and downtown Sapporo on a fixed schedule, making a car rental optional rather than essential.

## Getting There from Sapporo and Beyond

From New Chitose Airport, the most direct route is a taxi or rental car covering roughly seventy minutes via the Doto Expressway, bypassing central Sapporo entirely. Travelers arriving by train should take the JR rapid Airport Express into Sapporo Station, then transfer to a Chuo Bus bound for Jozankei Onsen, a journey of about ninety minutes total including the connection. Many guests instead rely on the hotel's own reserved shuttle service from designated pickup points in central Sapporo, a convenient option that should be booked at the time of reservation. Those driving independently from Sapporo simply follow Route 230 south along the Toyohira River, a scenic forty-minute drive that itself serves as a gentle transition from city pace to onsen calm.
