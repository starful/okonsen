Stepping through the entrance of Kasho Gyoen feels like crossing an invisible threshold between Sapporo's brisk urban energy and the hush of a mountain gorge. The lobby is dim and deliberate, lit by paper lanterns and the soft glow of a hearth, while beyond the windows the Toyohira River churns over dark volcanic rock. There is no attempt to overwhelm guests with grandeur here; instead, the ryokan works in restraint, letting the sound of rushing water and the scent of cedar do the talking. Within minutes of arrival, the pace of the city falls away, replaced by something slower and more attentive — a staff member kneeling to offer tea, the creak of tatami underfoot, the distant call of a bird from the surrounding cedar forest. It is this immediate sensory shift, achieved with so little ornamentation, that makes Kasho Gyoen distinct among Hokkaido's hot spring retreats.

## A Valley Shaped by Fire and Water

Jozankei's story begins not with samurai lords or ancient pilgrims, as in many onsen towns of Honshu, but with a Buddhist priest named Bikuni, who is said to have discovered the springs in 1866 while wandering the mountains south of what would become Sapporo. The name "Jozankei" itself derives from a combination of characters referencing the mountain gorge and the founding priest, and the town grew slowly through the Meiji era as Sapporo developed into Hokkaido's administrative capital. Because Hokkaido was settled far later than the rest of Japan, Jozankei's onsen culture carries a distinctly pioneer-era character — pragmatic, communal, and deeply tied to the raw landscape rather than court ritual. Kasho Gyoen itself is a newer addition to this valley's long soaking tradition, built to honor that frontier spirit while offering the comforts of contemporary Japanese hospitality. Older residents of Jozankei still speak of the ryokan's founding philosophy: that a bath here should feel less like a resort amenity and more like a return to something elemental.

### The Gorge as a Living Backdrop

Unlike onsen towns built around flat basins, Jozankei sits inside a narrow river gorge, and Kasho Gyoen was positioned specifically to take advantage of this topography. The building steps down toward the water in tiers, so that nearly every guest room and bathing area faces the churning Toyohira River and the granite cliffs beyond it.

## The Waters: Mildly Alkaline and Deeply Restorative

The onsen water at Kasho Gyoen is classified as a **simple alkaline hot spring** (tanjun-sen), one of the most common yet highly prized spring types in Japan for its gentle effect on skin and its suitability for long, repeated soaking. The water emerges from the source at a moderate temperature and is only lightly adjusted before reaching the baths, preserving its natural mineral composition of sodium, calcium, and trace amounts of metasilicic acid. Bathers often describe the sensation as silky rather than sharp, with none of the sulfurous bite associated with more acidic springs elsewhere in Hokkaido, such as Noboribetsu.

### Rotenburo with a Gorge-Facing View

The ryokan's crown jewel is its open-air rotenburo, carved partially into stone and positioned directly along the riverbank, separated from the rushing water by only a low natural rock wall. In autumn, the surrounding maples turn a deep crimson that reflects onto the steaming water's surface; in winter, snow gathers silently on the cedar eaves above while the bath itself remains warm at around 40 to 41 degrees Celsius, creating the classic Hokkaido contrast of fire and ice. There is also an indoor cypress bath (hinoki-buro) for guests who prefer privacy or milder weather, its wood releasing a faint resinous fragrance as it steeps in the mineral water. Private family baths (kashikiri-buro) are available for reservation, ideal for couples or families traveling with young children who want the gorge view without the shared bathhouse setting.

## Rooms Built Around Wood, Stone, and Silence

Kasho Gyoen's guest rooms lean into a restrained, almost austere aesthetic that draws more from Hokkaido's northern minimalism than the ornate lacquerwork of Kyoto ryokan. Walls are finished in unbleached plaster, floors alternate between tatami and dark wood, and large windows are treated as the primary decoration — each room angled to frame either the river gorge or the surrounding cedar forest. Furnishings are kept deliberately spare: a low chabudai table, cushions in muted indigo or charcoal, and futon bedding laid out each evening by staff, following traditional practice.

### Room Categories and Private Baths

Several room types include their own semi-open-air bath fed by the same mineral spring as the communal facilities, allowing guests to soak privately at any hour while still hearing the river below. Higher-tier rooms add a small tatami lounge area with a low sofa facing the window, better suited to lingering over tea after a long soak. Wabi-sabi principles are evident throughout — a deliberately uneven ceramic washbasin, a single ikebana arrangement rather than a full floral display, wood grain left visible rather than lacquered smooth. Nothing is polished to perfection, and that imperfection is precisely the point.

## Kaiseki Cuisine Rooted in Hokkaido's Terroir

Dinner at Kasho Gyoen is served as a multi-course kaiseki meal that leans heavily into Hokkaido's reputation as Japan's most productive agricultural and fishing region. The meal typically opens with a sakizuke amuse-bouche featuring seasonal vegetables from nearby farms, followed by a sashimi course built around Hokkaido staples such as sea urchin, scallop, and seasonal salmon or tuna, depending on the month. A standout course often involves Jozankei-area beef or Hokkaido pork, prepared as a tabletop shabu-shabu or grilled course, accompanied by locally foraged mountain vegetables like fuki and warabi in season.

### Breakfast: A Quiet Counterpoint

Breakfast shifts tone entirely, served either in-room or in a communal dining space depending on the room plan, and centers on a traditional washoku spread — grilled fish, simmered vegetables, house-made tofu, rice cooked in a clay pot, and miso soup made with regional miso paste. It is a deliberately gentle meal, designed to ease guests back into the day after a long soak and a substantial dinner the night before, and pairs beautifully with the Hokkaido-grown coffee or roasted hojicha tea often offered alongside it.

## Beyond the Ryokan: Sapporo and the Jozankei Valley

One of Kasho Gyoen's greatest practical advantages is its proximity to Sapporo, making it feasible to combine deep relaxation with genuine sightseeing rather than choosing one or the other.

### In the Immediate Area

Jozankei itself offers several short hiking trails along the gorge, including paths to viewpoints overlooking the Toyohira River's rock formations, particularly striking during the autumn foliage season from late September through mid-October. The Jozankei Free Footbath near the town center is a pleasant, no-cost way to sample the region's spring water before or after a proper soak. In winter, the valley becomes a gateway to nearby ski areas, with several resorts accessible within a thirty-minute drive.

### A Short Trip to Sapporo City

Central Sapporo is roughly a 60 to 75-minute drive or shuttle bus ride away, making it entirely feasible to visit Odori Park, the Sapporo TV Tower, or the historic Clock Tower on the same day as check-in. Travelers visiting in winter should time their stay around the Sapporo Snow Festival in early February, when the city's illuminated ice sculptures pair beautifully with an evening return to Kasho Gyoen's warm baths.

## Practical Guidance for Your Stay

### Tattoo Policy

As with most traditional ryokan in Japan, visible tattoos are generally restricted in the communal baths due to longstanding cultural associations. Guests with tattoos should not be discouraged, however — the private family baths and in-room open-air baths offer a complete workaround, and it is worth confirming policy details directly with the ryokan when booking.

### Best Season to Visit

While Jozankei rewards visitors year-round, autumn foliage (late September to mid-October) and deep winter snow season (January to February) are widely considered the two most visually spectacular windows, each offering a dramatically different rotenburo experience.

### Booking Tips

Because of its proximity to Sapporo, Kasho Gyoen books quickly during the Snow Festival period and Golden Week; reserving at least six to eight weeks ahead is strongly advised for those windows. Rooms with private open-air baths sell out first, so travelers prioritizing an in-room soak should book several months in advance where possible.

## Getting There

From New Chitose Airport, the journey to Jozankei takes approximately 90 minutes by rental car or airport limousine bus with a transfer in central Sapporo. From Sapporo Station, direct shuttle buses run several times daily to Jozankei Onsen, taking roughly 60 to 70 minutes depending on traffic and season; many ryokan, including Kasho Gyoen, can arrange private pickup for an additional fee. Drivers coming from Sapporo should follow Route 230 south, a scenic route that itself offers glimpses of the gorge before arrival.
